When Will the Muniaissance Reach Cincinnati?

November 11, 2026

Over the past two decades, the golf world has put a premium on great golf course architecture. Country club restorations and architecturally sound destination properties have begun to highlight designs from the early 1900s. The work of Donald Ross, Seth Raynor, Alister MacKenzie and others is finally being realized as intended. Many of their original designs were lost to wars, tight budgets and neglect. Bringing them back has deepened how golfers engage with and study the courses they play.

This hasn’t happened only at private clubs and resorts. Municipal golf is having a renaissance across the country. The focus on sound architecture has reached the public golf scene in cities like West Palm Beach, Charleston, Philadelphia, D.C., Nashville and Kansas City. The internet has dubbed this the “muniaissance,” as great architecture reaches the public in creative ways. One thing I keep wondering is when a municipal restoration will reach Cincinnati.

Work is already underway on Cincinnati’s public courses. The Cincinnati Recreation Commission is three years into a renovation campaign across its six Troon-managed courses: Woodland, Neumann, Reeves, California, Glenview and Avon Fields. The courses are funding the project themselves, with no outside investment, under a campaign called “Better Golf in Progress.” Its goal is to improve conditions, operations and the overall experience, with new carts, restrooms and irrigation systems, among other upgrades. This may modestly improve each CRC course, but it still leaves Cincinnati without a strong public golf identity on the national stage. Outside the city, these six courses have little to no reputation that sets them apart. One of them, however, has huge upside thanks to its golf history, architectural history and philanthropic work: Avon Fields.

Perched among the Cincinnati hills, Avon Fields is the oldest public course west of the Alleghenies. It opened in 1914. Its original design plans were lost, so its architecture remains a mystery. The design has been credited to Donald Ross, to Langford and Moreau, and to lesser-known architects. In its early years, Avon served as a public home for private golf groups, including bankers and professional baseball players. The CRC acquired the course in 1928 and built the clubhouse that same year. Almost a century later, that clubhouse still stands.

Avon was the home course of Jimmy Woods, an African American professional. Area clubs denied Woods access because of his race, so he played the public courses. He is credited with beating Arnold Palmer in an exhibition match when Palmer toured through the city, and Avon’s driving range is named in his honor. Woods’s protégé, Ron Dumas, is a member of the African American Golf Hall of Fame and has served as a golf professional for the CRC since the late 1960s. Through his nonprofit, Reaching Out for Kids, he has helped arrange hundreds of scholarships across the country while teaching underprivileged kids literacy, math and golf.

Anything still standing after a century deserves a celebration. Paired with Dumas’s work for the city’s children, Avon Fields is overdue for a campaign, and for facilities that match its mission. Two completed projects show how a city’s architectural legacy and philanthropy can come together.

Charleston Municipal

In 2021, golf course architect and realtor Troy Miller led a renovation of the course locals call “Muni,” in honor of Seth Raynor’s legacy in Charleston. The Lowcountry has almost no elevation, so the renovation drew on the bold features and template holes Raynor designed at Charleston Country Club and Yeamans Hall. Muni has no official tie to Raynor; the renovation was an homage to his legacy. It gave the public access to an architectural style that had been available only at private clubs. The Charleston community rallied behind Miller’s effort through a nonprofit called Friends of Muni, which was formed to fund the restoration. It also held volunteer days to plant trees that volunteers can now revisit when they play.

The Park, West Palm Beach

A run-down golf park was transformed into a state-of-the-art public golf facility that has become a national model. As in Charleston, the effort was community-led, spearheaded by Seth Waugh, CEO of the PGA of America. The community raised $56 million to fund it. Gil Hanse renovated the former Dick Wilson course, giving it broad appeal across West Palm Beach. He worked from design plans by Dirk Ziff, a Palm Beach County native, a sign of how deeply the project was rooted in the community. The Park puts a heavy emphasis on youth activities and education, and its clubhouse was renovated with that purpose in mind. The Park calls this initiative “The Path.” It offers free education, life skills and golf to the community, taught by golfers and teachers in subjects ranging from financial literacy to art to math.

Proposed Avon Fields

Avon Fields has the chance to pay a similar homage by adopting the architectural style of Cincinnati’s prominent clubs. Donald Ross designed Hyde Park, Maketewah and Kenwood, all of which have been restored in the past five years. Ross-style green pads and strategic bunkering would give Avon, a quaint par 66, wonderful flair. Better yet, the course could draw on the Langford and Moreau influence found at Clovernook Country Club. Clovernook features large back-to-front greens with severe drop-offs. High-IQ golfers will notice that Avon already has greens like these, most notably at the par-3 eighth. Hole 8 is a decaffeinated version of a standard Clovernook green, but it still poses a unique challenge. Blending both styles would bring fun updates to a historic public course and help Cincinnati grow its golf identity.

Reaching Out for Kids currently operates out of a trailer. I volunteered there a few times this year, and alarmingly, there were almost no other volunteers. An updated facility would better reflect Dumas’s intentions and his current work. It shouldn’t serve kids only on Saturday mornings; it should host after-school programs for kids in the area.

Avon Fields is set to receive new irrigation in 2027. What if, alongside that work, there were an effort to draw up course plans, raise money and imagine what Avon Fields could truly become?
